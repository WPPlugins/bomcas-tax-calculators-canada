## What is taxed

A capital gain is the proceeds of a capital property minus its cost and the costs of selling. The inclusion rate in the rate file is {rate:house.gains.inclusion}. That share of the gain is added to income. The rest is not taxed as income. A loss can offset a gain under the usual capital-loss rules. This page does not apply a capital loss against employment income.

A principal residence is not given an inclusion. The principal-residence exemption is not calculated here, because the form does not know which years a property qualified.

## How the calculation works

The taxable portion is the gain multiplied by {rate:house.gains.inclusion}. That amount is then taxed at the federal and provincial rates that apply to your other income. A person whose other income is already in a high bracket pays more tax on the same gain than a person whose other income is low. The province matters because provincial rates differ. British Columbia’s top rate is {rate:BC.income.top}. Alberta’s first rate is {rate:AB.income.first}. The gain itself is not a provincial program.

## Worked examples

A gain that is included at {rate:house.gains.inclusion} is only partly in income. If the gain is small and other income is inside the first British Columbia band, the provincial tax on the included portion starts from {rate:BC.income.first}, after the basic personal amount of {bpa:BC} has been considered on the return as a whole. The form uses the income you type. It does not assume the basic amount is still available if your other income has already used it.

The same included gain, stacked on income that already reaches the top band, is taxed at the marginal rate of that band, not at the average rate of the person’s whole income. Doubling the gain doubles the included amount. It does not double the tax if the extra dollars cross into a higher band.

A sale of a home that qualifies as a principal residence for the years you owned it is not this calculation. Entering the gain anyway overstates tax.

## Common mistakes

Including the whole gain instead of {rate:house.gains.inclusion}. Taxing a principal residence. Using one province’s brackets for a person who is taxed in another. Forgetting that the inclusion rate, not the provincial rate, decides how much of the gain enters income.

## Questions

### Is half always the included share?

The included share is the rate in the file, {rate:house.gains.inclusion}. Read that rate. Do not memorize a fraction if the file changes.

### Does the province change the inclusion rate?

No. The province changes the tax on the included amount. The inclusion rate is federal.

### Are capital losses subtracted from salary?

Not on this page. Capital losses offset capital gains, with the inclusion applied to the net gain under the usual rules.

### Is a principal residence a capital gain I should type?

Not if the exemption covers it. The form will not test the exemption for you.

### Why do two people pay different tax on the same gain?

Because their other income and their province put the included amount into different brackets.
